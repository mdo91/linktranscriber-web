## Prepare the complete comparison

Damas supports public, on-demand YouTube, Instagram and X video links up to 90 minutes. Process only material you are authorized to use. Private, restricted and live videos are unsupported; public sources can also be inaccessible.

1. Keep the original link and lecture title. Choose one analogy you want to understand.
2. Provide the supported link in Damas and follow the current transcription and built-in summary instructions, reviewing AI consent.
3. Wait for the results. Read the explanation before and after the comparison, including later qualifications. The overview may omit the analogy or its limit.
4. Replay the relevant speech. Check which terms belong to the familiar example and which belong to the idea being explained. Inspect diagrams yourself and label screen-only information separately.
5. Edit and copy the checked passages with surrounding words that help you find them again.

## Map relationships before shortening them

Create four fields in your own note: the idea being explained, the familiar comparison, the relationship explicitly shared, and the limit stated in the lecture. Attach a checked passage to each correspondence. If the lecturer pairs two elements but says nothing about another feature, mark that feature “not mapped”; do not transfer it merely because it belongs to the familiar example.

Distinguish a stated limit from your own concern about the analogy. Both can be useful, but only the first can be attributed to the lecturer. If no limit is stated, write that observation rather than claiming the comparison works in every respect. An analogy helps explain a relationship; resemblance alone does not verify the claim. Record any independent evidence separately.

## Fictional example: an outline as a travel itinerary

This is an invented teaching scenario, not a real lecture, quotation or Damas test. Imagine a lecturer compares an essay outline to an itinerary: sections resemble stops, transitions resemble connections, and the comparison concerns the reader’s route rather than the time spent writing.

| Familiar side | Idea being explained | Supported relationship in this fictional scenario |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stops | Essay sections | Parts of a planned route |
| Connections between stops | Transitions | How one part leads to another |
| Travel time | Writing time | Not mapped; explicitly outside this comparison |

A suitable short paraphrase of this fictional lesson is: the itinerary image explains the planned sequence of sections and their connections, without specifying writing time. It does not show that every essay needs the same route or that a planned structure guarantees a good essay. Those conclusions have no support in this scenario.

## Write the explanation without the image

Hide the familiar comparison and explain the actual idea in one sentence. Then add the analogy as an illustration and keep the stated limit. If you can only repeat the image, return to the explanation; do not invent a missing correspondence to complete the table.

Before copying the note, check both sides, every relationship, the stated limits and any unresolved wording. Keep the source link. Use quotation marks only for exact speech checked in the recording. The [claims and reasons guide](/guides/lecture-argument-map/) helps separate illustration from supporting evidence; use [quotation checking](/guides/verify-interview-quotes/) for exact words.
