To summarize a lecture definition, use the Damas transcript and built-in summary to locate the explanation, then list the conditions the lecturer actually states. Test each example against those conditions before writing a shorter definition. Keep three possible results: fits the stated definition, fails a stated requirement, or cannot yet be decided. You make this table yourself from editable, copyable text.

## Prepare the definition in context

Damas accepts supported public, on-demand YouTube, Instagram and X video links up to 90 minutes. On iPhone, iOS 18.6 or later, internet, Premium and consent for AI processing are required. Process only material you are authorized to use. Private, restricted and live videos are unsupported; a public source may also be inaccessible.

1. Keep the lecture link and title, and choose one term you need to understand.
2. Provide the supported link in Damas and follow the current transcription and built-in summary instructions, reviewing AI consent.
3. Wait for the results. Use the overview to find the term, then read its complete explanation and later qualifications.
4. Replay the definition, checking negatives, quantities and words such as “both”, “either” and “only”. Inspect diagrams yourself; label information read only on screen.
5. Edit and copy the checked passages. Keep enough surrounding words to find them again in the source.

## Separate the rule from the examples

In your own note, record the term, the scope of the definition, each required condition and its supporting passage. Preserve whether conditions must hold together or are alternatives. A feature shared by the lecturer’s examples is not automatically a requirement.

Check whether the lecturer gives a complete definition or only a necessary condition. If the source says a condition is required, satisfying it alone may not be enough to qualify. If it merely gives one example, do not invent a complete rule from it. Write “definition incomplete in this recording” and retain the question.

For each case, mark every condition present, absent or unknown, with evidence. Missing information is unknown, not absent. Use “fits” only when the source supplies enough of the rule and the case supplies all the required information. Use “excluded” only when a stated requirement demonstrably fails.

## Fictional exercise: a ready kit

This invented teaching example is not a source quotation or a real Damas test. Suppose a training lecture defines a “ready kit”, for that exercise only, as a kit containing both a brush and a tray. The exercise supplies the following cases:

| Case | Brush | Tray | Result under this exercise’s complete rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Present | Present | Fits |
| B | Present | Absent | Excluded: the tray requirement fails |
| C | Present | Not described | Undecided: check the tray information |

Do not summarize the definition as “a kit with a brush”. That loses the joint requirement and wrongly admits B. Do not exclude C merely because its description omits the tray. The next useful question is whether the tray is present, not whether the brush is suitable.

These are invented cases for learning the method. In your real note, label cases you invent as your own exercises, separate from examples actually given in the lecture. Never attribute your cases, results or wording to the lecturer.

## Write and test the short explanation

A summary of the fictional rule could say: within this exercise, both items are required; a missing tray excludes a case, while an unreported tray leaves it undecided. Retain the scope “within this exercise”; it is not a general standard for real kits.

For your source, keep the short definition, checked rule passages, case table and original link together. Hide the results, classify the cases again, then compare each decision with its evidence. If you cannot justify one, reopen that line rather than adding a condition from memory. This is a manual study exercise, not an automatic Damas score or a guarantee of learning.
